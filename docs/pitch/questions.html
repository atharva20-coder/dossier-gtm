Dossier · the debrief

Questions About Your Thinking

Everything they could reasonably ask, from “so what is it?” to the ones designed to make you squirm. Each answer has a line you can say out loud, the reasoning in points, the proof to point at, and the questions that come next.

Read this bit first — it explains the whole page.

  • They grade three things: does it work, why did you make those choices, and can you explain it to someone non-technical.
  • Their words: “the ones who do well aren't the ones who build the most — they're the ones who make the best choices about what to build.”
  • So nearly every question is secretly the same question: why that, and not something else?
  • A good answer has two halves — what you did, and the option you rejected. No rejected option in the answer makes it sound like you only thought of one thing.

The page is organised two ways at once. Sections run roughly easy → hard. And it deliberately covers all six angles an interviewer can come from: the facts, the risks, the upside, the alternatives, the gut feel, and how you worked. If a question isn't here, it's probably a reworded version of one that is.

level 1The warm-up

First five minutes. Low stakes, but they set the tone — and a rambling answer here costs you the room before you've started.

tests · can you be brief

So, what did you build?

Say this“You give it a name and a company. It reads about forty pages, picks the single best reason to contact that person, and writes the email — showing you the source for every claim.”
  • Two sentences. Then stop.
  • The silence after is theirs to fill, and what they ask next tells you what they actually care about.
  • Do not list features here. There will be twenty minutes for that if they want it.
Most common failure: answering this question for four minutes. They asked for a description, not a tour.
Why answer in two sentences

Because the first answer sets the level for everything after it. A long one signals you can't tell what matters, and it hands them no thread to pull on. A short one makes them choose the next question — and what they choose tells you whether this room cares about the mechanism, the commercial case, or the judgment.

tests · do you know your own scope

Which problem statement did you take?

Say this“PS-3 — personalised outreach, prospect to draft.”
  • Then one sentence on why, because they'll want it anyway: “because the hard part isn't the writing, it's finding something worth writing about.”
  • Don't apologise for not doing the others.
Why not hedge or apologise

Because choosing is the graded act. The brief explicitly rewards people who make good choices about what to build, so treating your own choice as something to justify defensively undercuts the thing being measured. State it, give the one-line reason, move on.

tests · basic orientation

Who is it for?

Say this“Anyone who sends cold outreach and cares whether it lands. Sales reps at volume, and founders doing their own selling.”
  • Those two get opposite value from it, and saying so shows you've thought about it.
  • A rep with 200 prospects: value is speed. They weren't going to research at all.
  • A founder with 20: value is quality. They'll edit every word — and those edits are exactly what it learns from.
Why name two users who want opposite things

Because one persona is easy to design for and proves nothing. Naming a high-volume rep and a founder — where one wants speed and the other wants care — shows you've thought about who the product isn't optimal for, and it sets up the honest answer later about adoption and CRM integration.

tests · can you sequence a demo

Show me.

Say this“Let me show you a finished one first, then run a live one.”
  • Never let them watch a loading spinner in the first thirty seconds. Have a completed lead open before you start talking.
  • Point at things in this order: the hook → its source link (click it) → the findings column → the research map.
  • Then untick a fact and hit Rewrite. That single action explains the whole design better than any sentence.
  • Only then run something live.
Proof — the four runs worth rehearsing
  • A finished lead (Maheedhar Maram · Zamp) — 58 pages, 42 seconds.
  • Michael Chen, no company → it stops and asks.
  • Harshad Vaidya · Kelphi Systems → nothing real found, and it says so.
  • Three API keys deliberately broken → the run still finishes.
Why show a finished run before a live one

Because the first thirty seconds decide whether they're watching the tool or watching a progress bar. A finished run puts the reasoning on screen immediately; the live run then proves it wasn't staged. Doing it the other way round spends your best minute on a loading state.

tests · honesty about scale

How long did this take you?

Say this“A week. Most of it went on one problem — deciding which fact to open with.”
  • Don't inflate it and don't undersell it.
  • The interesting part isn't the duration, it's the distribution: searching was solved in a day, writing is a prompt, and the remaining days all went on judgment.
  • That split is the answer to “did you spend your time on the right thing”.
Why the split matters more than the total

Because a week is a week — the number carries no information. Where the week went does: one day on retrieval, an afternoon on drafting, and the rest on choosing. That distribution is a direct answer to “did you spend your time on the right thing”, which is the question actually being asked.

level 2How you framed the problem

The first genuinely revealing questions. They're checking whether you solved the brief as written, or the real problem hiding behind it.

tests · did you read past the brief

Why did you pick PS-3?

Say this“Because the slow part isn't the writing. It's finding something worth writing about — and most tools in this space are built as if writing were the problem.”
  • The brief describes a salesperson who can't spend 20 minutes per prospect.
  • Obvious reading: make the writing faster. That's the cheap half.
  • The expensive half is the research — hunting for one real, current reason to contact this person, then turning scattered notes into a sentence.
  • I picked it because the obvious answer and the right answer are different. That's where there's something to prove.
Proof — what to point at on screen
  • A finished run: 58 sources read in about 50 seconds, 39 of them about the person, not the company.
  • The findings column: 29 facts considered, one picked, every rejection showing its reason.
  • Writing is the last and shortest step — roughly 10 of those 50 seconds. The other 40 is the part a rep skips when they're busy.
Then they'll ask
“Wouldn't PS-1 have been safer?”
Yes. Invoices have a right answer you can check. I picked the one where “correct” is a judgment call, because that's the harder thing to show.
“How do you know research is the slow part?”
I timed myself doing it by hand. Finding one real, current, relevant signal took about 40 minutes across LinkedIn, X and news. Writing from those notes took 4.
Why the harder problem was the right choice

Because PS-1 and PS-2 have checkable answers — an invoice either reconciles or it doesn't — so a working build proves the build works and nothing more. PS-3 has no ground truth, which means every design decision is an argument you have to be able to defend. That's the only one of the three where the reasoning is visible in the output.

tests · do you know what you're optimising

What makes a “good” hook? How did you decide?

Say this“Recent, about the person and not their company, and connected to what I'm selling. In that order.”
  • A hook = the one fact the email opens with. The reason you're writing today.
  • I started with just two rules: recent and specific. It gave me hooks that were true and useless — a 10-year-old research paper, a head-office address.
  • What was missing was the sender. It was picking the most interesting fact about the person, not the one that gives you a reason to write.
  • Once what I sell feeds into the ranking, the exact same set of facts produces a different, better answer.
Proof — real numbers from this build
  • Across all runs: 1,114 sources read, 307 from LinkedIn or X — by far the two biggest.
  • 58% of chosen hooks came from LinkedIn or X. 12 of 24 were about the person, not their employer.
  • One run threw away six IEEE papers — genuinely his, but 10 to 12 years old. True, correctly matched, and still no reason to email him.
They'll push: “How do you know it's better?” Be straight — you can't measure reply rates on a case study. What you can measure is how often a run finds a person-level hook instead of a company-level one. That's the early signal worth watching.
Then they'll ask
“Who decides what's relevant — you or the AI?”
Both, in order. Fixed rules cut 30 facts down to 8. One AI call picks the winner from those 8. The rules are readable and the AI shows its reason, so a rep can argue with either.
“What if someone sells something unusual?”
Then it adapts without touching code — the product and the problem you solve are settings that feed the ranking. A recruiter and an investor want opposite things from the same facts.
Why the sender belongs in the ranking at all

Because “most interesting fact about this person” and “best reason for me to contact them” are different questions, and only the second one produces a reply. A ten-year-old research paper wins the first and loses the second. Feeding what you sell into the ranking is what turns a research tool into an outreach tool.

tests · scope judgment

What did you decide not to build?

Say this“A sequencer, a CRM connector, and bulk sending. The first two for time. The last one on purpose.”
  • Sequencer + CRM connector: both are the right next things. Both are plumbing — connecting to other tools. They'd have eaten the week and shown nothing about how the app decides.
  • Bulk send: left out deliberately. A button that emails 40 strangers at once is a different product with a much bigger blast radius.
  • The safety rail is worth more here than the feature.
Proof — what I built instead
  • A CSV export with exactly the columns a sequencer wants: email, where the email came from, subject, body, segment, and whether it's already been sent.
  • Sending is one person at a time, with a confirm step, on both screens.
  • Re-researching someone does not wipe the “already emailed” record. Looking someone up again should never make you forget you already contacted them.
Then they'll ask
“What if a customer demanded bulk send?”
I'd build it, but with a review step — every draft approved before the batch goes out. The point isn't to be slow. It's that nothing unreviewed leaves the building.
“What else got cut?”
Follow-up sequences and a per-lead to-do queue. Both real, both needed data I hadn't designed, and neither would have shown anything new about judgment.
Why bulk send was refused rather than deferred

Because the other two cuts were about time and would be built next week unchanged. Bulk send is a decision, not a backlog item: the moment one click can reach forty strangers, every other guard in the system becomes advisory. Saying “I ran out of time” would have been the easier answer and the false one.

tests · problem decomposition

How did you break the problem down?

Say this“Into seven steps, where each one can fail on its own and say why.”
  • 1 · Who are they? Confirm the right person before spending anything.
  • 2 · Read. Search their LinkedIn and X specifically, plus news, plus a profile service.
  • 3 · Pull out claims. Turn pages into ~30 separate facts.
  • 4 · Check each one. The claim must appear word-for-word on the page.
  • 5 · Rank. Sort by tier, then score inside the tier.
  • 6 · Decide. One AI call over the final 8.
  • 7 · Write, then verify the email still contains the fact.
Proof
  • Each step writes itself to the database the moment it finishes — so a half-failed run leaves a readable record instead of a spinner.
  • That's why the walkthrough page can show real timings per step: research 19.0s, extract 11.7s, check 0.2s, write 10.6s.
Then they'll ask
“Why seven and not one big AI call?”
Because one big call can't tell you where it went wrong. Seven steps means a failure has an address. It also means six of the seven are cheap and predictable.
Why seven steps rather than three

Because each of the seven is a place where the run can fail for a different reason the user needs to hear — wrong person, nothing found, found but unverifiable. Collapsing them would produce the same output with a vaguer explanation, and the explanation is the product.

level 2The facts and figures

The data questions. Know these cold — hesitating on your own numbers reads as not having looked.

tests · do you know your own system

How long does one prospect take, and what does it cost?

Say this“About 50 to 60 seconds, roughly 15 searches, and zero pounds — everything's on free plans.”
  • Time: ~42s on the run I'd demo, ~60s typical.
  • Searches: ~15 per prospect. That's the real constraint, not compute.
  • Money: £0. Every service used has a free tier and none is required.
  • By hand, the same work is about 40 minutes.
Proof
  • One campaign used 59 searches — the app counts them itself, because the search provider offers no way to ask what's left.
  • Four contacts researched at once in 98 seconds, versus about four minutes one after another.
Then they'll ask
“What would it cost at real scale?”
Search is the line item. At 15 searches a prospect, a team doing 1,000 a month needs a paid search plan — that's the first bill, and it's small next to a rep's hour.
“Why is search the bottleneck and not the AI?”
Because one run makes a handful of AI calls but fifteen searches. The reading is the expensive part, the thinking isn't.
Why searches are the number to quote, not seconds

Because seconds are cheap and get cheaper, while searches are a hard external allowance that someone eventually pays for. Quoting the constraint that actually binds — rather than the one that sounds impressive — is what makes the cost answer credible at scale.

tests · white-hat rigour

What data do you actually have to back any of this up?

Say this“Everything a run does is written to a database, so all my numbers are read back out — none of them are estimates.”
  • 1,114 sources read across all runs. 307 from LinkedIn or X.
  • 58% of chosen hooks came from LinkedIn or X.
  • 12 of 24 hooks were about the person rather than their employer.
  • 214 design decisions recorded with their reasons.
  • What I don't have: reply rates. Nobody replied, because this is a case study and I sent one email.
Say the missing number out loud. Volunteering “I have no reply data” before they ask is worth more than any figure you do have. It tells them you know which numbers matter.
Then they'll ask
“So how would you know if it worked?”
Reply rate against a templated control group, split by whether the hook was person-level. That's the experiment I'd run first with real traffic.
“And before you have that?”
Watch the share of runs producing a person-level hook, and how often a rep edits the opening sentence. Both are available today.
Why volunteer the missing number

Because they'll work out you have no reply data within one follow-up, and finding it themselves is much worse than hearing it from you. Naming the gap first proves you know which metric would settle the argument — which is more valuable than any figure you do have.

tests · does the accuracy claim hold up

How accurate is it?

Say this“I can tell you precisely how often it makes something up: never, by construction. I can't tell you how often it misreads something — and that's a different problem.”
  • Inventing facts: blocked. A claim must appear word-for-word on the source page or it's dropped. On the demo run, 18 of 18 passed that check.
  • Misreading facts: still possible. A real sentence can be understood wrongly, and no string match catches that.
  • Wrong person: caught by two filters, both added after it happened.
  • So the honest claim is narrow and provable, not broad and vague.
Then they'll ask
“What's your error rate?”
I don't have one, and I'd distrust anyone who quoted a single number for this. The failures are different kinds, and they need different fixes.
Why refuse to give a single accuracy number

Because “accuracy” bundles three unrelated failures: inventing a fact, misreading a real one, and matching the wrong person. They have different frequencies, different fixes, and different costs. One number would average them into something that sounds precise and means nothing.

tests · technical literacy

What's it built with, and why those?

Say this“Boring choices everywhere except the ranking, which is where the interesting problem was.”
  • Backend: Python and FastAPI. Postgres for storage.
  • Frontend: React and TypeScript.
  • AI: Gemini, asked to return structured data rather than prose so the output can be checked.
  • Search: Tavily. Profiles: a person-data provider. Emails: Hunter plus a verifier. Sending: plain Gmail.
  • Nothing exotic. The judgment went into what to build, not into picking unusual tools.
Then they'll ask
“Why not an agent framework?”
Because I wanted every step to be inspectable and to fail with a specific reason. Frameworks hide the loop, and the loop is the thing I needed to reason about.
“Why not a vector database?”
There's nothing to search over. Every run fetches fresh pages about one person and throws them away. A search index solves a problem I don't have.
Why boring tools everywhere else

Because every hour spent on an unusual stack is an hour not spent on the ranking problem, which is the only part where the output would differ from anyone else's build. Familiar tools also fail in familiar ways, which matters when you're debugging under a deadline.

level 3Why those edge cases

The brief says the edge cases you pick reveal how well you understand the problem. Expect them to dig here.

tests · do you know where it really breaks

Why these edge cases and not others?

Say this“Because each one is a way of being confidently wrong — and that's the only kind of failure that reaches a real person.”
  • A slow run is annoying. A crash is obvious — you see it and fix it.
  • But an email about the wrong Michael Chen reads perfectly. So does congratulating someone on their colleague's award. Both land in a stranger's inbox, under your real name.
  • I picked the failures that look like success, because nobody catches those.
  • And I didn't pick them upfront — every one actually happened while building. A namesake's podcast got chosen as a hook. An intern's award nearly went out to an executive. They picked themselves.
Proof — run these live in the demo
  • Michael Chen, no company given → finds 4 believable people, stops and asks, spends nothing.
  • Harshad Vaidya · Kelphi Systems → 44 sources read, 15 facts about a different Harshad thrown out, nothing good survives, so it writes a generic email and tells you it's generic.
  • Three API keys deliberately set to nonsense → the run still finishes, and the log says “Hunter rejected the key — check it has not expired”.
Then they'll ask
“What edge cases did you consider and reject?”
Hitting a rate limit, a broken CSV upload, a very long name. All real, all boring — the app behaves the same either way. I kept the ones where the output changes, not the plumbing.
“What if the person has no LinkedIn at all?”
It falls back to news and the open web, and tells you that's what happened. If nothing personal survives, it uses a company fact and labels it as one, rather than faking something personal.
“What if two of these happen at once?”
They're separate checks, so they stack. The Harshad run is two at once — the wrong-person filter and the nothing-found path firing together.
Why failures that look like success are the ones worth building for

Because visible failures get fixed by whoever hits them — a crash reports itself. A fluent, confident, wrong email reports nothing; it just quietly costs you a relationship and never appears in a bug tracker. Choosing the silent failures means choosing the ones that would otherwise survive indefinitely.

tests · do you understand cost asymmetry

Two people share a name. Why stop and ask instead of just picking the likelier one?

Say this“Because getting it right saves four seconds, and getting it wrong costs you the relationship. A confidence score doesn't know that.”
  • Guess right → you saved about four seconds. Nice, not important.
  • Guess wrong → a beautifully researched email about a total stranger, sent under your name, to someone who spots it instantly.
  • Those two outcomes are wildly unequal, and no percentage score captures that. So it has to be a rule, not a threshold.
  • Same thinking when it finds nothing: write something generic and say it's generic, rather than invent a reason.
Proof
  • It only stops when two candidates are genuinely believable. Give it a company name and it resolves instantly at 100% confidence — no interruption on the normal path.
  • Answering the question is cheap. It picks up where it left off instead of starting over.
  • Same rule for job changes: it only reports a new employer when the person is confirmed and confidence is 75+. A shaky match claiming a new job is usually the wrong person, not real news.
Then they'll ask
“Doesn't that jam a batch of 200?”
Only the unclear ones stop; the rest finish. At volume that becomes a short to-do list — short, because most rows already come with a company attached.
“Where's the threshold, and why there?”
Deliberately cautious, and I'd tune it once I had real data. Today I'd rather ask too often than send one wrong email.
Why a rule instead of a confidence threshold

Because a threshold assumes the cost of being wrong is roughly constant, and here it isn't — one outcome saves four seconds and the other burns a relationship permanently. No probability number encodes that asymmetry, so the decision has to live outside the score. This is the one place where I deliberately overrode a model's own judgment with a flat rule.

tests · can you reason about a limit you can't remove

What happens when the research is simply wrong?

Say this“Sometimes it will be. The design assumes that instead of pretending otherwise.”
  • Every claim gets checked back against the actual page it came from. That stops the AI inventing things.
  • It does not stop misreading. A real sentence can still be understood wrongly.
  • So the human is built into the loop: every fact shows its source, you can untick any of them, and nothing ever sends by itself.
  • The system is designed to be corrected, not trusted.
Proof — demo the correction loop
  • Untick a fact, hit Rewrite. The email rebuilds from what's left — no new searching, no extra cost.
  • Every fact has a clickable source. Open one mid-demo. The claim is right there on the page.
  • Your corrections teach it: three consistent edits become a standing rule, visible in Settings with the evidence behind it.
Then they'll ask
“How would you catch a misreading at scale, when nobody's checking?”
You wouldn't, reliably. That's exactly why nothing sends automatically. The constraint is doing the work, not a confidence score.
“Has it actually been wrong?”
Yes, three times, and I can name them: a namesake's podcast, an intern's award, and a hook about a company he'd already left. Each one became a check.
Why design for correction instead of chasing accuracy

Because the remaining failure — misreading a real sentence — has no technical fix I could build in a week, and probably none at all. Given a limit you can't remove, the honest response is to make it cheap to catch and cheap to undo: sources on every fact, a rebuild that costs nothing, and a human between the draft and the send.

tests · edge cases you haven't hit yet

What edge case are you sure exists that you haven't found?

Say this“Two real people with the same name at the same company. Every filter I have would let that through.”
  • The wrong-person filter works by checking the source ties the person to the company. Two people at one company both pass.
  • Rare, but a large company makes it likely — and it would produce a perfectly confident wrong email.
  • Second one I'd expect: a person who just changed jobs, where LinkedIn is updated and news isn't. Half the facts describe a job they no longer hold.
  • Third: someone whose name is also a common word or a company name. Search noise would swamp them.
This question is a gift. Naming a hole you haven't fixed is a stronger signal than listing the ones you have. It proves you're still looking.
Then they'll ask
“So why haven't you fixed it?”
I only found it by thinking about it, not by hitting it, and the fix needs a signal I don't have — a job title or a profile URL to separate them. I'd rather name it than paper over it.
Why name an unfixed hole out loud

Because a list of solved problems only proves you stopped looking. Naming one you can't yet fix — and saying precisely what signal the fix would need — shows the search is still running, which is the behaviour that predicts what happens after you're hired.

level 3Risk, legal, and what could go badly wrong

The cautious angle. Somebody in that room is paid to worry, and these are their questions.

tests · do you know the legal edges

Are you scraping LinkedIn? Is that allowed?

Say this“No. I search LinkedIn and X the same way a search engine does, and read what comes back publicly. I never log in and I never get past a login wall.”
  • Be precise here — “I scrape LinkedIn” is both wrong and the answer that gets you caught out.
  • What actually happens: a search restricted to those sites, plus a profile data provider that supplies its own data.
  • The hard part was never fetching pages. It was validating what came back.
  • If they push on the provider: it's their data and their terms, which is a commercial question rather than a technical one.
Never claim capability you don't have. Claiming access past a login is exactly the kind of overreach the rest of the tool exists to prevent — and it's easy to disprove in the room.
Then they'll ask
“What about GDPR?”
Business contact details for B2B outreach generally sit under legitimate interest, but it needs a lawful-basis record, an opt-out, and a deletion path. I have the deletion path — a lead can be removed with its research. I don't have the rest, and I wouldn't ship to an EU customer without it.
“Could someone ask you to delete their data?”
Yes, and it works today, because everything about a person hangs off one lead record.
Why the precise wording matters so much

Because “I scrape LinkedIn” is both untrue and instantly checkable, and one overclaim contaminates everything else you've said about not making things up. Describing exactly what happens — a site-scoped search plus a data provider — is also the stronger answer, since validating what comes back was the hard part, not fetching it.

tests · do you understand the real-world blast radius

What's the worst thing this could do?

Say this“Send a fluent, confident, well-researched email about entirely the wrong person, under a real name, to someone who spots it immediately.”
  • Note what's not on that list: crashing, being slow, writing an awkward sentence. None of those reach anybody.
  • The damage isn't technical. It's a burnt relationship and a rep who looks careless.
  • Every guard in the app points at that one outcome: same-name filter, colleague filter, source check, human review, no bulk send.
  • Second worst: quietly using a fact about a company the person left, and being slightly wrong in a way nobody notices.
Then they'll ask
“Has that happened?”
Not to a real recipient. It happened three times in testing, before send, which is why the filters exist.
“What stops it now?”
Nothing sends automatically. That single constraint does more than every filter combined, and I'd keep it even at the cost of throughput.
Why the worst case isn't technical

Because crashes and slow runs never reach a person, and everything that does reach a person is a relationship. Ranking the failures by who they touch rather than by how loud they are is what puts human review above every clever filter.

tests · security instinct

Where do the credentials live?

Say this“In the environment, never in the database and never behind a form in the app.”
  • The Gmail app password is send access to a real mailbox. Putting it behind a settings form puts it one bug away from being read back out.
  • The app shows you whether something is configured, never the value.
  • Signing in uses the sending account's own credential — so whoever is signed in is demonstrably the person the emails come from.
  • The comparison is constant-time on both halves, so the address can't be guessed by timing it.
Proof — a real catch before the first push
  • A secrets scan found an access key hardcoded in a test file. It never reached the repository.
  • The repository is private, and the environment file is excluded from it.
Then they'll ask
“What would you change for production?”
A proper secret manager rather than environment variables, OAuth instead of an app password, and per-user credentials rather than one shared mailbox.
Why a settings form would be the wrong place

Because anything the app can write into a database, some future bug can read back out. An app password is send access to a real mailbox — the blast radius of leaking it is someone else emailing as you. Keeping it in the environment means the app can prove it's configured without ever being able to reveal it.

tests · do you think about the recipient

Isn't this just a better spam machine?

Say this“It's the opposite bet. Spam is cheap volume. This makes each message expensive enough that you'd rather send twenty good ones than two hundred bad ones.”
  • It refuses to write something personal when it can't find anything real, and labels the generic version as generic.
  • There's no bulk send. One person per request, with a confirmation.
  • ~15 searches per prospect is a real cost, which naturally pushes toward fewer, better targets.
  • The volume play is the thing this is competing against, not the thing it enables.
Don't get defensive. The honest version is stronger: any tool like this could be used for volume. The design decisions push the other way, and you should be able to name them.
Then they'll ask
“What if a customer used it for volume anyway?”
They'd hit the search budget fast, and the app would tell them. But ultimately a determined user can misuse any tool — what I can control is the default, and the default is careful.
“What about deliverability?”
Not solved, and I'd flag it as a real gap. Sending volume, domain warm-up and bounce handling are their own discipline, and pretending otherwise would be the same overreach I've avoided elsewhere.
Why the cost per message is the defence

Because spam economics depend on marginal cost being near zero. Fifteen searches and a human read per message is a real cost, and it pushes a user toward fewer, better targets whether they intended that or not. Design that makes the bad use expensive works better than a policy asking people not to.

tests · dependency risk

What happens if one of your providers disappears tomorrow?

Say this“The run still finishes. I tested that deliberately with three keys set to garbage.”
  • Every outside service is optional. None is required for a run to complete.
  • Missing key, wrong key and out-of-credit all behave identically — only the reason shown differs.
  • Search is the one real dependency. Lose that and quality drops sharply, though the app still runs on the profile provider alone.
  • Nothing spends credit on a page load. Only a deliberate click costs anything.
Proof — measured, not claimed
  • Three broken keys → run completed, receipt read “Hunter rejected the key — check it has not expired”.
  • Four page loads → zero provider calls.
  • Forced to zero search budget → the run still found contacts and worked out addresses, and said “stopped researching, 0 searches left this month”.
Then they'll ask
“Why did you bother with that?”
Because free tiers run out on the day you need them, and because an expired key that reports itself as “no match” is the one message that stops someone fixing it. I hit exactly that bug.
Why treat every provider as optional

Because free tiers run out on the day of the demo, and a hard dependency turns a small outage into a dead product. It also forced a better failure message: an expired key that reports itself as “no match” is the one wording that stops a user fixing it, and I only found that by deliberately breaking the keys.

level 4Where you changed your mind

The highest-value question in the whole interview — and the one most candidates have no real answer to.

tests · can you tell when you're wrong

Walk me through a decision you reversed.

Say this“Ranking. I tried to fix it with maths four times before I accepted that maths was the wrong tool for the job.”
  • Original approach: score every fact on how recent it is, how good the source is, how relevant it is. Highest score wins.
  • Each fix produced a different wrong winner — a work anniversary, then a decade-old exam result, then a job at a company he'd left.
  • Four tries in, the number of tries was the evidence. Scores let a well-documented trivial fact climb past a plainly better one, because you're comparing across categories that aren't comparable.
  • Replaced it with a plain ordering: 1) things they said themselves, 2) their current job, 3) everything else, 4) a former employer. The tier beats the score outright.
  • Then one AI call over the final 8. “Which of these is worth opening with?” isn't a maths question.
Why an ordering beats a score

A score assumes every fact sits on one shared scale. They don't. “He posted about X last week” and “his old employer won an award” aren't two points on the same line — they're different kinds of thing, and forcing them onto one number is what let the award win. Tiers say the quiet part out loud: some kinds of fact are simply better openers than others, regardless of how well evidenced they are.

Proof — the four failed attempts, in order
  • Facts with no date scored 0.35 and beat dated ones — so the version of an event that told you less kept winning.
  • A “ignore words under 3 letters” rule was silently dropping “AI” — the single most important word in the offer.
  • 4 of 13 dated hooks carried today's date, because the extractor stamps “now” when it can't find a real one — and “today” scores maximum freshness.
  • The person-level bonus was being applied to other — the bucket that literally means “couldn't classify this”.
Say the number out loud. “Four attempts” is the part that shows judgment. “I iterated on the ranking” tells them nothing.
Then they'll ask
“How did you know the approach was wrong, not just the numbers?”
Because each fix gave a different wrong answer, not a closer one. Getting closer would have meant I was tuning. Jumping around meant the whole model was wrong.
“Then why keep any scoring at all?”
Because it's cheap, predictable and explainable, and it narrows 30 facts to 8. Only the last comparison needed real judgment — so only that part gets an AI call, and the other 29 comparisons stay free and auditable.
“And if that AI call is wrong?”
It's allowed to say “none of these”, and any failure falls back to the ranked order. Switch it off entirely and the app still produces an answer — which is why it's an improvement rather than a dependency.
tests · do you revisit assumptions

What did you learn about the problem that you didn't know at the start?

Say this“That most facts on the internet have no usable date — which quietly breaks any system built on recency.”
  • I assumed dates would just be there. About a third of facts have none.
  • Worse: some arrive stamped with today's date, because when the extractor can't find a date it defaults to “now”.
  • And “today” scores maximum freshness. So the facts it couldn't date were beating the ones it could.
  • Fix: distrust any date that's today or later, and rank undated facts by how well-evidenced they are instead.
Why not just drop undated facts?

Because that would throw away roughly a third of everything found, including good material — LinkedIn rarely dates a role change in the text, and press coverage often doesn't either. Deleting them optimises for tidiness at the cost of coverage. Ranking them lower keeps them available when nothing better exists, which is exactly the situation where you need them.

Proof — measured, not guessed
  • 4 of 13 dated hooks in the database carry the date of the run that created them.
  • Undated facts are now separated by how many independent websites report them, and by wording — “just launched” counts, “will launch soon” gets marked down.
  • If the date field is empty it reads the year out of the sentence — “in 2014”, “since January 2023” — but only after a word like “in” or “since”, so a headcount never gets mistaken for a year.
Then they'll ask
“What's the proper fix?”
Have the extractor return how sure it is about the date, instead of just a date or nothing. That's a bigger data change than I had the week for. What's there now is a workaround, and I'd replace it.
“Doesn't distrusting today's date lose genuine same-day news?”
Yes, and I made that trade knowingly. One genuinely fresh fact gets demoted, versus a third of all dates being wrong the other way. The asymmetry decides it.
tests · intellectual honesty

What's the assumption you're least sure about?

Say this“That a fact about the person always beats a fact about their company. I built it that way and I'm not certain it's true.”
  • It's baked into the ordering, and I do believe it as a default.
  • But I've seen it break in my own output. If someone's company just moved its entire product onto AI agents, that is a far better opener than anything personal.
  • Right now the ordering is absolute — a company fact can never jump the queue.
  • A more honest design would let a strong enough company signal cross the line. I don't yet know where that line sits.
Why I shipped it as an absolute anyway

Because the alternative — a threshold I'd have picked from intuition — is a made-up number wearing a lab coat. A hard rule is at least honest about being a rule, and it's visibly wrong in a way I can point at. A soft threshold would be wrong in ways nobody could see, and I'd have no evidence to defend the value I chose.

Proof — the counter-example is in the app
  • On the Zamp run, four company facts scored within 0.01 of each other — a product launch, an expansion, and the move to AI agents. Maths can't separate those. A human obviously can.
  • The final AI call overruled the top-scoring one and chose the AI-agents fact, saying it matched what the sender sells. That's the line being crossed by judgment instead of by rule.
Then they'll ask
“So is the ordering wrong?”
Right as a default, wrong as an absolute. It stops a former employer beating a post from last week — a real bug it fixed. It just shouldn't be unbreakable.
“How would you find the right line?”
From replies, not from my gut. Until there's outcome data, a firm default plus a judgment override is the honest position.
tests · can you separate signal from noise in feedback

What feedback did you get, and what did you ignore?

Say this“The most useful correction I got was ‘don't exclude anything — learn to prioritise'. It changed the whole ranking model.”
  • My instinct was to delete weak facts: too old, wrong company, gone.
  • The correction was that deleting throws away context. An old role at a different company is a bad opener and excellent background — it's what tells you the new SVP has done this before.
  • So age became a penalty that keeps shrinking but never reaches zero, and career history is kept and passed to the writer as background, clearly fenced off.
  • What I didn't act on: suggestions to add more sources. Coverage wasn't the problem — choosing was.
Why a penalty rather than a cutoff

A cutoff has to pick a day where a fact stops mattering, and no such day exists — a two-year-old funding round matters less than a two-week-old one, but not zero. A decaying penalty encodes “less useful over time” without ever claiming “worthless after date X”, and it means an old fact can still win when nothing newer exists, which is the only time you'd want it to.

level 4The roads you didn't take

The creative angle. They want to know whether you considered other designs, or just built the first one you thought of.

tests · did you consider alternatives

Why not just hand the whole thing to one big AI call?

Say this“Because when it's wrong, you get one wrong answer and no idea which part failed.”

  • Seven steps means every failure has an address: it couldn't confirm the person, it found nothing, it found things but none survived the source check.

  • It's also cheaper. Six of the seven steps are plain code, so only the two that need judgment cost anything.

Because a rep who can't tell why the tool was wrong stops trusting it entirely after the first bad result. Explainability isn't a nicety here — it's what determines whether the thing is still in use in week three.

Then they'll ask
“Isn't that more code to maintain?”
Yes, and worth it. The alternative isn't less code — it's the same complexity hidden inside a prompt where you can't test it.
tests · do you know when not to use AI

Why not fine-tune a model?

Say this“Because my problem isn't that the model writes badly. It's that it doesn't know anything true about this person.”
  • Fine-tuning teaches style. Style was never the bottleneck — a prompt handles it.
  • The bottleneck is fresh, specific, checkable facts, which no amount of training gives you.
  • It would also need training data I don't have: hundreds of examples of the right hook chosen from a real fact set.
  • And it makes the output less explainable, which is the opposite of what this needs.
Why the persona learns by rules instead

Because style adaptation needs to be reversible and visible. A learned rule can be read, argued with and undone; a fine-tuned weight can't. And it works from three examples rather than three hundred — which matters when your user is one salesperson, not a data-labelling team.

Then they'll ask
“When would fine-tuning be right?”
Once there are thousands of sent-and-replied emails per customer, and the goal shifts from “sound like them” to “sound like what works”. That's an outcome-data problem, and I don't have outcome data.

Why build this at all? Clay and Apollo exist.

Say this“They're mostly data plus templating. The part I built is the part they leave to you — deciding which fact is worth opening with.”

  • What they hand back is a spreadsheet with more columns. Choosing the angle is still your job.

Because enrichment is a commodity — several companies sell the same data — while judgment is not. If you can already buy the columns, the defensible work is what you do with them. That's also the part that improves with use, because it learns from which hooks a specific person actually sends.

Then they'll ask
“So would you integrate with them rather than compete?”
Yes, and that's the honest answer. Let them supply the list and the contact data; this decides what to say. That's a better product than either half alone.

If you couldn't use AI at all, what would you build?

Say this“A change detector. Watch a list of people, and tell a rep the day something happens.”

tests · design imagination

What's the version of this you'd build with no constraints at all?

Say this“It wouldn't be an app you open. It would be a queue that tells you who to write to today, and why.”

  • The email is the output. Any tool can produce one.
  • The findings column is the reasoning: 18 facts, one chosen, 16 rejected with reasons.
  • It's the only screen that lets you judge whether the tool was thinking.
  • And it's the screen that makes the tool correctable rather than something you either trust or don't.
Why show the working when most users only want the answer

Because trust is built at the moment the tool is wrong, not the moment it's right. A rep who sees a bad hook and can immediately see why it was picked learns where to rely on it. A rep who just sees a bad email learns to stop opening the app.

level 5The hardest ones

Designed to knock you off balance. The content matters less than staying calm and not getting defensive.

tests · composure under dismissal

This is just a wrapper around an LLM, isn't it?

Say this“The LLM part took an afternoon. The week went on the parts that stop it being confidently wrong — and those are ordinary code, not prompts.”
  • Don't get defensive, and don't over-argue. Agree with the true half.
  • Then be specific: the same-name filter, the colleague filter, the word-for-word source check, the date distrust, the tiered ranking. None of those is a prompt.
  • The AI is used twice in seven steps, and both uses are checked by code afterwards.
  • Offer the test: “turn the AI ranking call off and it still produces an answer. That's how much of this isn't the model.”
Why agreeing first is the strong move

Because the accusation is partly true, and denying it makes everything after sound defensive. Conceding the easy half buys the right to be precise about the hard half — and the precision is what actually answers the question.

tests · can you take a direct hit

I ran it and the hook it picked was bad. Explain that.

Say this“Show me the findings column and I'll tell you exactly why it picked that — and whether it's a bug or a bad rule.”
  • Don't explain in the abstract. Open the screen.
  • Every rejection has a recorded reason, so this is diagnosable in front of them — which is itself the answer.
  • Most likely causes, in order: the fact had no date so it ranked on evidence instead; the person-level tier beat a better company fact; or relevance to what you sell didn't reach the ranking step.
  • All three are known, and the third is the one I'd fix first.
Why this question is an opportunity

Because being able to explain a bad output on the spot, from the record, is a stronger demonstration than any good output. It proves the reasoning is stored rather than reconstructed — and that's the claim the whole design rests on.

Never say “that shouldn't happen”. It did happen; they're looking at it. The right move is to diagnose it live.
tests · intellectual honesty under pressure

Convince me this isn't a solution looking for a problem.

Say this“The problem is that research is skipped, and you can verify that without me — look at your own inbox this morning.”
  • Evidence you can point at: every templated cold email anyone receives is a rep who skipped the research step.
  • The tool doesn't create demand for personalisation. That demand is already why reply rates are what they are.
  • What's genuinely uncertain isn't the problem — it's whether this is the right shape of solution, versus something living inside the CRM.
  • Concede that second point. It's the honest half and it's also my roadmap.
Why separate the two halves

Because “is this a real problem” and “is this the right solution” get bundled into one dismissal, and they deserve different answers. The problem is verifiable today. The solution shape is genuinely open — and saying so is more persuasive than defending both with equal confidence.

tests · can you be wrong gracefully

I disagree with your ranking model. Personal facts aren't always better.

Say this“You're right, and I have a run that proves it. It's the assumption I'm least sure about.”
  • Do not defend it. Agreeing here is both honest and stronger.
  • The evidence is already in the app: four company facts within 0.01 of each other, and the AI call overrode the tier to pick one of them.
  • What I'd defend is the default: it stops a former employer beating last week's post, which was a real bug.
  • What I wouldn't defend is making it absolute.
Why concede rather than argue the point

Because they may well be testing whether you can be moved by an argument. Defending a position you've already documented as uncertain would contradict your own record — and the record is on the table. Conceding the exact right amount, and naming what you'd still defend, is the harder and more useful skill.

tests · self-knowledge

What would someone who worked with you last week say you got wrong?

Say this“That I kept tuning something that needed rethinking, and that I overwrote two files without checking they existed.”
  • Both are specific, both are true, and both have a stated fix.
  • The tuning one: four attempts where two would have been enough.
  • The files one: recovered fully, and the project is in version control now.
  • Avoid the disguised-strength answer. “I care too much about quality” fails this question outright.
Why two rather than one

Because one sounds curated. Two, from different categories — a judgment error and a carelessness error — reads as an actual assessment rather than a prepared line. And naming a carelessness error is the harder one to volunteer, which is what makes it credible.

tests · the real question underneath

Why should we hire you for this role?

Say this“Because the thing this role does all day is decide what's worth building, and that's what the last week was — including the four times I decided wrong and changed direction.”
  • Point at the specific evidence rather than claiming a trait.
  • I found the failures by running the thing and being unsatisfied — not by reviewing code.
  • I can explain it to a rep in two sentences and to an engineer in five.
  • And I can tell you what's wrong with it, precisely, without being asked.
Why lead with the reversals rather than the build

Because everyone in the final round has a working build. The differentiator is whether you can show the decisions behind it — and a documented reversal is the only kind of evidence that can't be produced by someone who got lucky.

level 4What you'd do next

They're checking whether you can prioritise when everything is possible.

tests · prioritisation

One more week. What do you build?

Say this“Follow-ups, then CRM write-back. Not more research.”
  • Research quality is already good enough to judge. That's not where the gap is.
  • What's missing is what happens after the first email — a second and third message that know what the first one said and how long ago.
  • And getting the result into the tool the rep already lives in.
  • Replies come from persistence far more often than from a better opening line.
Why follow-ups before CRM write-back

Because follow-ups multiply the value of every run I already do, whereas write-back only moves existing output somewhere else. And the groundwork is already there — sends are recorded per lead, and the runner-up hooks are already discarded, so a second message has both the history and the material without new research.

Proof — the groundwork is already in
  • Every send is recorded per lead with the email, subject and time — so a follow-up would already know what was said and when.
  • The app already throws away the runner-up hooks. A second message would use the best unused one instead of repeating itself.
Then they'll ask
“Why not better research?”
Diminishing returns. A first email that's 10% better matters less than a second email existing at all.
tests · scale thinking

I give you two hundred prospects tomorrow. What happens?

Say this“It runs. The search quota stops it, not the code.”
  • Roughly 15 searches per prospect. 200 prospects = 3,000 searches. That's past the free tier.
  • Runs already happen several at a time, but capped — so it goes fast without hammering anything.
  • The app counts its own usage and stops cleanly with a reason, instead of dying halfway through.
  • At that size the interesting question changes. Not “can it write 200 emails”, but “which 20 deserve a human's attention today”.
  • The app already produces that ranking. I'd make it the main screen.
Why the question changes at that volume

Because at 200 a day the bottleneck moves from the machine to the person. Drafting 200 emails is easy; reading 200 is not — and nothing sends unread. So the useful work becomes triage, and the app already has the inputs for it: how well someone fits the ideal customer, and whether a personal hook was found at all.

Proof — measured, not projected
  • Four contacts researched at once in 98 seconds, versus about four minutes one after another.
  • One campaign used 59 searches. The app counted them itself and warns before the month runs dry.
  • Forced down to zero budget, the run still finished — contacts found, emails worked out, and a note saying “stopped researching, 0 searches left this month”.
Then they'll ask
“What breaks first?”
The search quota, then wall-clock time. The database is barely working by comparison.
“How do you pick the 20?”
A fresh job change first — strongest reason to write today, and it goes stale in weeks. Then how well they fit the ideal customer, combined with whether there's a personal hook at all.
tests · can you see past your own build

Six months in. What does this look like?

Say this“Not an app you open. A queue that tells you who to write to today, sitting inside the CRM.”
  • Today you bring it a name, which means you've already done the hard targeting work yourself.
  • The version that earns daily use watches a list and surfaces the people where something changed this week.
  • The draft becomes a by-product of the alert rather than the destination.
  • And by then it should be learning from replies, not just from edits — which is the only feedback that says whether any of this works.
Why the shape has to change and not just the features

Because the current shape asks the rep to remember to use it. Every tool that depends on being remembered loses to the one that interrupts you at the right moment. Moving from pull to push isn't a feature — it's the difference between a tool that's tried and a tool that's adopted.

tests · does the learning claim survive scrutiny

You say it learns. Prove it.

Say this“Open Settings. Every learned rule shows the evidence it came from, and you can delete any of them.”
  • Two separate things are learned. How you write — from edits you make to drafts. What you write about — from which hooks you actually send.
  • It waits for three consistent edits before writing a rule. One edit is a mood; three is a preference.
  • Only costly actions count. A send counts double a hand-pick. A draft you ignored teaches nothing.
  • It only ever promotes, never demotes — because not doing something isn't evidence you dislike it.
Why ignoring a draft has to count for nothing

Because most drafts get ignored for reasons that have nothing to do with quality — the rep got busy, the lead went cold, the day ended. Counting inaction as dislike would teach it that everything is bad, and it would slowly converge back onto whatever the defaults already were. Only actions with a cost carry information.

Proof — visible in the app
  • Two hand-picked speaking hooks moved that category to ×1.2, rising to ×1.4 once campaign sends were counted too.
  • On two facts of equal age, the defaults pick the promotion 0.3725 to 0.3353. With learning applied, the talk wins 0.4023 to 0.3725 — the order flips.
Then they'll ask
“What if it learns something wrong?”
You delete the rule, and rules are versioned so it's one click. A setting you state explicitly always beats a rule it inferred — a statement outranks a guess.

Notice the pattern running through every answer: a specific failure, a rejected alternative, or a reason you can point at. That's not decoration. It's the difference between describing what you built and showing how you decided.
