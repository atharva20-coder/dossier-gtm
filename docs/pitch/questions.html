Dossier · the debrief

Questions About Your Thinking

The brief says they grade the judgment behind your choices, and that the debrief is a conversation about your thinking rather than a second test. These are the questions that actually asks.

What they said they care about: whether the process works, the judgment behind your design choices, and how you explain it to a non-technical buyer. Also: “the ones who do well aren't the ones who build the most — they're the ones who make the best choices about what to build.”

So almost every question will be a version of why did you choose that. The strongest answer names the alternative you rejected and what it would have cost. An answer with no rejected option in it sounds like you only thought of one thing.

How you framed the problem

The first and most revealing questions. They are checking whether you solved the brief or the real problem behind it.

tests · did you read past the brief

Why did you pick PS-3?

Because the bottleneck in it isn't writing, and every tool in that space is built as if it were.

The brief describes an SDR who can't spend twenty minutes a prospect. The obvious reading is “make the writing faster”. But writing is the cheap half — the expensive half is finding a reason to write at all, and then re-reading your own notes to turn it into a sentence. I picked it because the obvious solution and the correct one are different, which is where there's something to prove.

tests · do you know what you're optimising

What does “good” look like for a hook? How did you decide?

Recent, about the person rather than their employer, and connected to what the sender sells. In that order.

I started with just recent and specific, and it produced hooks that were true and useless — a decade-old research paper, a headquarters address. The missing dimension was the sender: the system was optimising for the most interesting fact about a person rather than the one that gives you a reason to write. Once the sender's product and problem feed into ranking, the same fact set produces a different, better answer.

Follow-up to expect: “How do you know it's better?” Be honest — you can't measure reply rate on a case study. What you can measure is the share of runs producing a person-level hook rather than a company-level one, and that's the leading indicator worth watching.
tests · scope judgment

What did you decide not to build?

A sequencer, a CRM connector, and bulk sending — the last one deliberately, not for lack of time.

The first two are the right next things and both are integration work rather than judgment work: they'd have eaten the week and demonstrated nothing about how the process decides. Bulk send I left out on principle. A campaign that can mail forty strangers on one click is a different product with a different blast radius, and the guardrail is worth more than the feature.

Why those edge cases

The brief says the edge cases you choose tell them how well you understand the problem. Expect this to be pressed.

tests · do you know where it really breaks

Why these edge cases and not others?

Because each one is a way of being confidently wrong, and confidently wrong is the only failure that reaches a real person.

A slow run is annoying. A crash is visible. But an email about the wrong Michael Chen, or congratulating someone on a colleague's award, is fluent, plausible, and lands in a stranger's inbox under a real name. I picked the failures that look like success, because those are the ones nobody catches.

They also weren't chosen in advance. Every one is something that actually happened while building — a namesake's podcast got picked as a hook, an intern's award nearly went out to an executive. They chose themselves.

tests · do you understand cost asymmetry

When two people share a name, why stop and ask instead of picking the most likely?

Because the two outcomes cost wildly different amounts and the confidence score doesn't know that.

Guessing right saves four seconds. Guessing wrong produces a well-researched email about a stranger, sent under your name, to someone who can tell immediately. That asymmetry doesn't show up in a confidence number, so it has to be a rule. The same logic decides what happens when nothing is found — it writes a generic message and labels it generic, rather than inventing something to say.

tests · can you reason about a limit you can't remove

What happens when the research is just wrong?

Sometimes it will be, and the design assumes that rather than pretending otherwise.

Every claim is checked back against the retrieved page, which stops invention but not misinterpretation — a real sentence can still be read wrongly. So the human stays in the loop by construction: every fact is shown with its source, any of them can be excluded and the message rebuilt, and nothing sends by itself. The system is designed to be corrected, not trusted.

Where you changed your mind

The highest-signal question in the whole interview, and the one candidates most often have no answer to.

tests · can you tell when you're wrong

Walk me through a decision you reversed.

Ranking. I tried to solve it with arithmetic four times before accepting the arithmetic was the wrong instrument.

Facts get scored on recency, source quality, relevance. Each fix produced a different wrong answer — a work anniversary, then a decade-old exam result, then a role at a company he'd left. Four attempts in, the number of attempts was the evidence: a multiplier lets a well-evidenced trivial fact climb past a plainly better one, because the comparison is between facts, not within them.

So I replaced it with an explicit ordering — what they said themselves, then their current job, then anything else, then a former employer — where the band beats the score outright. And then one final judgment across the shortlist, because “of these eight, which is worth opening with” isn't an arithmetic question.

Say the number. “Four attempts” is the part that shows judgment. Saying “I iterated on the ranking” conveys nothing.
tests · do you revisit assumptions

What did you learn about the problem that you didn't know at the start?

That most facts have no usable date, which quietly breaks the thing recency-based ranking rests on.

I'd assumed dates would be there. About a third of extracted facts have none, and worse, some arrive stamped with today's date because the extractor defaults to “now” when it can't find one — and today scores maximum freshness. So the facts it could not date were outranking the ones it could. The fix was to distrust any date on or after the run date, and to rank undated facts on how well evidenced they are instead.

tests · intellectual honesty

What's the assumption you're least sure about?

That a person-level hook is always better than a company-level one.

It's baked into the ordering and I believe it, but I've seen counter-examples in my own output: for someone whose company just moved its whole product onto AI agents, that company fact is a far better opener than anything personal. The current design treats the tier as a hard ordering. A more honest version would let a strong-enough company signal cross the band boundary, and I don't yet know where that threshold sits.

Explaining it to someone non-technical

Graded explicitly. They may literally ask you to do it in the room.

tests · can you drop the jargon

Explain what this does to an SDR who's never used an AI tool.

“Give it a name and a company. It does the reading you don't have time for, then writes the email — and shows you the page every claim came from.”

Then the one rule: it isn't allowed to make anything up. If it can't point at a page, the claim doesn't survive. Everything else in the build is machinery to enforce that sentence.

Don't say: pipeline, grounding, extraction, provenance, multiplier. If you use one, you've failed the question — and the question is being asked on purpose.
tests · commercial realism

Why wouldn't a rep just do this themselves? They're better at it.

They are better at it. They just don't do it — that's the whole problem.

A rep researching properly beats this every time on one prospect. But they have two hundred, so they don't research at all, they template it, and the reply rate reflects that. This isn't competing with a rep doing their best work. It's competing with the version of the email they actually send on a Thursday afternoon.

tests · do you know how tools like this die

Would a rep still be using this in week three?

Not as it stands — because it lives outside the tools they already work in.

That's the documented failure mode for this category: enthusiastic in week one, abandoned by week three, because reps live in Outreach and HubSpot and copying drafts across by hand is worse than not having the tool. The CSV export is the stopgap. Writing back into the CRM is the first thing I'd build with real budget, ahead of any new capability.

Trap: this is a test of self-awareness, not optimism. Answering “yes, they'd love it” fails it.

What you'd do next

They're checking whether you can prioritise when everything is possible.

tests · prioritisation

Another week. What do you build?

Follow-ups, then CRM write-back. Not more research.

Research quality is the part that's already good enough to judge. What's missing is what happens after the first message: a second and third touch that know what the first one said and how long ago, and a way for the result to land where the rep already works. One reply comes from persistence far more often than from a better opening line.

tests · scale thinking

I give you two hundred prospects tomorrow. What happens?

It runs, and the search quota is what stops it — not the code.

About fifteen searches a prospect, so two hundred is three thousand, which is beyond the free tier. Runs are already concurrent but bounded, and the app counts its own usage and stops cleanly with a reason rather than dying halfway. At that volume the interesting question stops being “can it draft two hundred” and becomes “which twenty are worth a human's attention today” — which is the prioritisation the ICP score and hook strength already produce, and which I'd make the main screen.

tests · where you spent the week

Where did most of your time go, and was that right?

Deciding which fact to open with — far more than on research or writing. And yes, though I'd get there faster now.

Retrieval was largely solved in a day. Writing is a prompt. The hard, ambiguous part — worth the time — was judgment: which of thirty facts deserves the first sentence, and how to be right about that for a person you know nothing about. What I'd change is stopping sooner when a tuning approach kept producing different wrong answers.

Nearly every answer here has a specific failure or a rejected alternative in it. That's not decoration — it's the difference between describing what you built and showing how you decided.
