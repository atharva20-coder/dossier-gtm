Dossier · demo narrative

What to Say at Zamp

Two things make this different. Lead with those, and let the machinery come out in the questions.

The brief from Zamp's GTM head

Don't walk in and list features. Pitch the idea. Two things make this different from everything else out there — say those, and let the mechanism come out in the questions.

So: steps 01 to 04 are the pitch. Everything from 05 onward is an answer you give when asked, not a script you work through.

01

The problem, from watching the job

Open here. Not with the product, not with the tech — with what you found when you looked at how GTM people actually spend a day.

Say

“I went and looked at where GTM teams actually lose their time, and it isn't writing. It's the research before it. Scrolling the web, LinkedIn, X — jotting things into a doc. Then later, opening that doc again and trying to write a message out of it.”

Say

“That's two separate costs. Finding the signal, and then re-reading your own notes to turn it into an email. Both take real effort, and the second one is why people stop doing the first.”

Then land the consequence, because it's the reason this whole category exists and mostly fails:

Say

“So most people skip it. They put the company name in a template and send it at scale, and the reply rate tells them exactly what the prospect thought of it.”

Why open with the problem and not the product

Because everyone in that room has seen a demo of an AI that writes emails. Nobody has heard someone explain which half of the job is actually slow. The problem is the part you observed; the product is just what follows from it — and leading with the observation is what makes the rest sound earned.

02

First difference: it isn't a web search

Get this sentence right. “AI researches your prospect” is what every single tool in this category claims.

Say

“This isn't a web search with a nice wrapper. It goes where a person actually leaves a trail — their LinkedIn, their posts on X — reads the pages, and checks every claim back against the page it came from. You get a real view of the lead, not a summary of their company's homepage.”

Then the half people miss, which is about who you contact rather than what you send:

Say

“And it works the other way round too. Give it a company and it finds the handful of people there actually worth a conversation — instead of running a campaign at everyone in the org and hoping someone bites. Quality leads, not volume.”

Numbers, only if they want them. All from real runs on this machine:

1,114pages read across all runs
307from LinkedIn or X — the two biggest by far
58%of chosen hooks came from LinkedIn or X
12 / 24hooks about the person, not their employer
Say it precisely — this one matters

Don't say “I scrape LinkedIn”. You don't, and if they ask how you get past the login you'll be caught.

Say instead: “a search restricted to LinkedIn and X, plus a profile provider, and every claim checked against the page it came from.”

Why that's also the better answer: fetching pages is easy. Validating what comes back is the hard part — so the accurate version is the more impressive one.

03

Second difference: the persona starts at fifteen percent, not zero

This is the one nobody else has. Spend the most time here.

Say

“Once the right leads are identified, it researches each one properly — who they are, what they've been posting on LinkedIn and X recently — and drafts the message. But the interesting part isn't the drafting.”

Say

“You design your own persona on day one. So you don't start at zero — you start at maybe fifteen percent, and it writes as you from the very first message rather than sounding like a language model.”

Say

“Then it closes the gap by watching you. Every time you edit a draft before sending, it looks at what you changed — and after a few consistent edits it writes that down as a rule and applies it from the next message on. You never train it. You just keep editing, which you were going to do anyway. And that's the point: you stay in the loop, nothing sends by itself.”

If they ask whether that's real or just a clever prompt, this is what's actually stored:

Show · Settings → Learned
  • Rules it worked out from your edits, each showing how many edits it took.
  • It waits for three consistent edits — because one edit is a mood, and three is a preference.
  • Rules get corrected, not stacked up: a new rule that contradicts an old one replaces it, and the set is capped.
  • A second list: which kinds of signal you actually act on, learned from what you sent and hand-picked — never from drafts you left alone.
Why ignoring a draft has to count for nothing

Because drafts get ignored when the day ends or the lead goes cold, not because they were bad. If inaction counted as dislike, it would slowly learn that everything is bad and drift back to the defaults it started with. Only actions that cost something carry information.

04

The GTM thinking underneath it

If they ask how you decided what to build, this is the answer. Four levers, ordered by how much they move whether a meeting gets booked:

LeverWhy it ranks there
TargetingA perfect email to the wrong person fails every time
TimingIs there a reason to reach out this week?
Offer relevanceDoes what I sell connect to what they're dealing with?
PersonalisationThe craft of the sentence — fourth, not first
Say

“Personalisation is fourth on that list. Everyone builds for fourth. The research is what gets you the first three.”

And signals aren't equal, either:

Signals, strongest first
  • A new exec hired into the relevant function. The strongest signal there is — new leaders change vendors in their first ninety days.
  • A job posting next to your pain. Badly under-used, and it's evidence of a real problem someone is spending money on.
  • A funding round. High intent, but saturated — everyone emails them that week.
  • An award. Says nothing about whether they need anything.
Say

“Most tools treat a hiring post and a ‘best places to work' award as the same kind of signal. One is evidence of a problem. The other is a press release.”

05

The build, in plain steps

Walk it once, out loud, before touching the screen. Seven steps.

What happens, in order
  • Work out who they are. Two believable people with the same name? It stops and asks rather than researching a stranger.
  • Go and read. Search LinkedIn, X, news and podcasts — anchored to the confirmed person.
  • Follow the threads the way a human would. But every search keeps their name in it, so it can't drift onto their employer's competitors.
  • Pull out the claims from what it read — roughly thirty of them.
  • Check each one. The specific things a claim names must appear word for word on the page. Deliberately dumb, because an AI can't argue its way past a text match.
  • Rank and pick. Sort into tiers, score inside each tier, then one judgment call over the final eight.
  • Write it in your saved voice — then check the finished email actually still contains the specific detail.
Say

“Every step saves itself to the database as it happens. So if it breaks halfway you get a readable record instead of a spinner — and the screen shows you the reasoning, not just the answer.”

Why seven steps and not one big AI call

Because one call means one failure mode: it produced something, and you can't tell which part went wrong. Seven means every failure has an address — couldn't confirm the person, found nothing, found things but none survived the check. Those are three different messages the user needs to hear, and one call can't distinguish them.

06

The demo, in order

Have a finished lead open before you start talking. Never make them watch a loading screen.

Show · lead screen
  • The hook first, with its source link underneath. Click through to the real page.
  • The findings column — say “this is every fact it considered, and why each one was kept or dropped.”
  • Open the research map. This is the moment that lands: it shows how it got there, step by step.
  • Untick a fact and press Rewrite. The message rebuilds from what's left. “The ranking is a set of rules. Rules are wrong sometimes, so you can overrule them.”
  • Press a tone chip — Casual, Shorter. Same facts, different style.
Show · competitor outbound
  • Type ramp.com. It finds who competes with Ramp, then the decision-makers at each — every one citing the page that named them.
  • Point at the receipts tab — every service's outcome, counted.
  • Open a contact, show the assembled message, then “open as lead” — a campaign contact is a fully researched lead.

07

The three things that prove it's real

Anyone can demo a happy path. Volunteer these before you're asked — each one is a real bug, found and fixed in the open.

It refuses to guess who someone is

A namesake's podcast appearance got picked as the reason to write to the wrong person. So now: a fact about someone who merely shares the name is dropped, and if two people are genuinely believable the run stops and asks.

It won't take credit for someone else's news

An intern's award at the same company was nearly used as a hook for an executive. Now every fact has to record whose it is, and one about a third party gets dropped at every stage.

It never dresses a guess up as a fact

When an address is worked out from a company's naming pattern rather than looked up, it says derived — on screen, in the tooltip, and in the CSV a sequencer imports.

Say

“The verifier came back ‘valid' for an address I made up, because that domain accepts all mail. If I'd trusted it, the tool would have certified a fabricated address as confirmed. It now spots that and refuses to claim it.”

Handle this carefully

Don't oversell the address finding. It's a free-tier email finder plus arithmetic, and it says so.

Why that restraint matters here of all places: claiming verified data you don't have is exactly the failure the rest of the tool exists to prevent. One overclaim contaminates everything else you've said — and they will spot it.

08

When they push back

“Why wouldn't a rep just use ChatGPT?”

Because it will invent the funding round. The whole system is the machinery that stops that: going and reading real pages, checking every claim word-for-word, and refusing to write when there's nothing real to say.

“Does this actually get used in week three?”

Be honest: most tools like this don't, because reps live in Outreach and HubSpot and copy-pasting forty drafts is worse than not having the tool. That's why there's a CSV export with the columns a sequencer imports — and why the next thing I'd build is writing back into the CRM, not more features.

“What would you do with real budget?”

Paid contact data for verified addresses, plus job-posting and exec-hire feeds — the two strongest signals, and the two that public search is worst at finding.

“What's it cost to run?”

Everything is on free plans, and the app respects them: nothing spends an allowance on a page load, only when a person asks for work. Every service is optional — no key, wrong key, and out of credit all end the same way, and the run tells you which happened.

If it breaks live

Say what happened and read the message off the screen.

Why that's the right move: a tool that reports its own failure clearly is the exact point you've been making for twenty minutes. Don't apologise for it — use it.

09

Close on the honest limitation

End on judgment, not features. It's the strongest note available and it can't be faked.

Say

“The thing I'd fix first isn't in the interface. It's that ranking still leans toward the most interesting fact rather than the one that best connects to what you sell. I know exactly where that change goes, it's small, and it has a big effect on quality. That's what I'd do on day one.”

Why finish on a flaw

Because every candidate ends on what their build does well, and it all sounds the same. Naming one precise, fixable weakness — with its location — is the only closing line that can't be rehearsed by someone who doesn't understand their own system.

Read the problem before the product. Show the reasoning, not just the answer. Volunteer the flaw before it's found.
