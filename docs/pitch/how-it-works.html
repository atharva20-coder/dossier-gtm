How Dossier Works

The whole system in plain language, the shape of it in two pictures, and why each part is built the way it is.

Explain it without a single technical word

Give it a name and a company. It goes and does the reading you don't have time for, then writes the email.

A salesperson researching one prospect properly spends about forty minutes: LinkedIn, X, the company site, a news article, notes into a document — then later they open that document again and try to write something from it. Two separate costs, which is why most people skip it and send the generic version.

This does the reading in about a minute, and hands back one specific reason to get in touch — with a link to the page it came from, so you can check it.

The one rule underneath everything: it is not allowed to make anything up. If it cannot point at a page for a claim, the claim does not survive. That constraint is what most of the machinery below exists to enforce.

The shape of it

Each part, and the failure that shaped it

Work out who they areidentity

Two people can share a name. Before spending anything, it confirms which one this is — and if two are genuinely plausible, it stops and asks rather than guessing.

Why: a namesake's podcast appearance was picked as the reason to write to someone who had never been on a podcast. Guessing here means researching a stranger and emailing a real person about them.

Go and readresearch

Searches scoped to their LinkedIn and X specifically, plus the open web, plus a profile provider. Every query keeps their name in it, so it cannot wander onto their employer's competitors.

Why: unanchored searching drifted onto Accenture, AWS and a university that merely appeared nearby. The anchor is the whole point.

Pull out claimsextract

Turns pages into individual factual statements, each carrying who it is about, which company, and the page it came from.

Why “who it is about” is a field: an intern's award at the same company was nearly used as the reason to write to an executive. Without recording the subject of a fact, there is no way to notice.

Check every claim against the pagegrounding

The specific things a claim names — a company, a number, a title — must appear word for word in the retrieved text. If they don't, the claim is dropped.

Why: a model will produce “raised $18M led by Accel” in perfect, confident English about a company that raised nothing. It is deliberately a dumb string match, because a model cannot talk its way past one.

Rank what is leftjudgment

Nothing is thrown away for being old. Everything stays in the list, and ranking decides — by how recent it is, whether it is about them or their employer, how many independent sources carry it, and whether it connects to what you sell.

Why nothing is excluded: an old fact is often the only thing that says who someone is. A ten-year-old VP role at a different company is a poor reason to write today and exactly what tells you the new SVP has done the job before.

Decide, once, over everythingthe collective call

The rules narrow the field to a shortlist. One judgment then looks at all of them together — with their dates, their sources, and what you sell — and picks the one to open with, or declines.

Why: scoring each fact alone and taking the highest kept producing a different weak answer — a work anniversary, then a decade-old exam result, then a role at a former employer. Four rounds of adjusting the arithmetic was the evidence that arithmetic was the wrong tool: the comparison is between facts, not within them.

Write it in your voicedrafting

Your persona — described once on day one — writes the message, then it is checked that the message actually used the specific detail rather than drifting into something generic.

Why the check: a rewrite can quietly lose the one specific thing and still read well. Then it is a generic email wearing a researched one's clothes.

Every provider is optional. No key, a wrong key and an exhausted quota all produce the same outcome — the run completes on whatever is still available — and the only difference is the reason shown. Nothing spends an allowance to draw a status badge; a check happens when a person asks for one.

If they ask what you'd need money for

  • Verified contact data. Addresses are currently found where possible and otherwise calculated from a company's naming convention — and labelled as calculated everywhere they appear.
  • Job-posting and executive-hire feeds. The two highest-intent triggers, and the two that public search is worst at surfacing.
  • Writing back into the CRM. Reps live in Outreach and HubSpot; a tool that needs copy-paste is abandoned by week three.

Every decision above was made because something broke first. That is the honest version of an architecture diagram — not a plan that survived contact, but the shape left behind after it didn't.
