How Dossier Works

The whole system in ordinary words, the shape of it in two pictures, and the thing that broke first behind every design choice.

The one-paragraph version

Give it a name and a company. It does the reading you don't have time for, then writes the email.

Doing this properly by hand takes about forty minutes per prospect — LinkedIn, X, the company site, a news article, notes into a doc. Then later you open that doc again and try to write something from it.

That's two separate chores, and the second one is why people give up on the first and send the generic version instead.

This does the reading in about a minute and hands back one specific reason to get in touch, plus a link to the page it came from so you can check it yourself.

One rule sits under everything: it is not allowed to make anything up. If it can't point at a page, the claim doesn't survive. Almost all the machinery below exists to enforce that single sentence.

Five words worth knowing first

These come up constantly. Learn these and the rest of the page reads easily.

Hookthe reason to write

The one fact the email opens with. “You just joined X.” “Your company just launched Y.” Everything the app does is really about picking a good one.

Facta single claim

One statement pulled out of one page, carrying who it's about, which company, and where it came from. A run produces about 30 of these. Exactly one becomes the hook.

Checking a claim“grounding”, if you meet the word

Before a fact is allowed anywhere near the email, the specific things it names — a company, a number, a job title — must appear word for word on the page it supposedly came from. If they don't, it's thrown out.

Banda tier, ranked above scoring

Facts get sorted into four groups by what kind of thing they are — their own post, their current job, other, former employer. A higher band always beats a lower one, no matter what the scores say.

Personathe voice it writes in

You describe yourself once on day one — who you are, what you sell, how you write. Then it learns the rest by watching which drafts you edit.

The shape of it

Read this picture left to right, then down. Facts only ever get fewer — never more.

The same picture in five lines

  • Read. Four kinds of source — their LinkedIn, their posts on X, news and podcasts, and a profile provider.
  • Pull out claims. Roughly 30 individual facts.
  • Throw out the bad ones. Four filters: wrong person with the same name, a colleague rather than them, a rival mentioned in the same article, and anything the page doesn't actually say.
  • Rank what's left. Nothing is deleted for being old — old facts stay in the list and simply lose.
  • Pick one, write, then check the email still contains it.

Each part, and the thing that broke first

Every one of these exists because something went wrong. None of it was designed up front.

1 · Work out who they areidentity
  • Two people can share a name. Before spending anything, it confirms which one you mean.
  • If two are genuinely believable, it stops and asks instead of guessing.

What broke: a different person with the same name had been on a podcast. That podcast became the reason to write to someone who had never been on one. Guess wrong here and you research a stranger, then email a real person about them.

2 · Go and readresearch
  • Searches aimed specifically at their LinkedIn and their X, plus the open web, plus a profile provider.
  • Every single search keeps their name in it. That's the trick — it can't wander off.

What broke: without that anchor, searching drifted onto Accenture, AWS and a university that just happened to be mentioned nearby. Keeping the name in every query is the whole fix.

3 · Pull out claimsextract
  • Turns pages into separate factual statements.
  • Each one records three things: who it's about, which company, and which page it came from.

Why “who it's about” is stored: an intern's award at the same company nearly became the reason to write to an executive. If you don't record whose fact it is, there's no way to spot that.

4 · Check every claim against the pagegrounding
  • The specific things a claim names must appear word for word in the text that was fetched.
  • If they don't, the claim is dropped. No appeal.

What broke: an AI will write “raised $18M led by Accel” in perfect, confident English about a company that raised nothing. This check is deliberately dumb — it's a plain text match, and an AI can't talk its way past one.

5 · Rank what's leftjudgment
  • Nothing is thrown away for being old. Everything stays in the list and ranking decides.
  • Four things move a fact up: how recent it is · whether it's about them or their employer · how many separate sources say it · whether it connects to what you sell.

Why nothing is excluded: an old fact is often the only thing that tells you who someone is. A VP role ten years ago at a different company is a terrible reason to write today — and exactly what tells you the new SVP has done this job before.

6 · Decide once, over all of themthe collective call
  • The rules narrow ~30 facts down to a shortlist of 8.
  • Then one AI call looks at all 8 together — dates, sources, what you sell — and picks the opener. Or declines.

What broke: scoring each fact on its own and taking the winner kept producing a different weak answer — a work anniversary, then a decade-old exam result, then a job at a company he'd left. Four rounds of tweaking the maths was the evidence that maths was the wrong tool. The comparison is between facts, not within one.

7 · Write it in your voicedrafting
  • Your persona writes the message.
  • Then it checks the finished email actually still contains the specific detail, rather than drifting into something vague.

Why that last check: a rewrite can quietly lose the one specific thing and still read beautifully. At that point it's a generic email wearing a researched one's clothes.

The one idea worth drawing twice

This is the bit most worth understanding, because it's where the biggest reversal happened.

  • Old way: give every fact a score, take the highest. Sounds sensible. Kept picking rubbish.
  • Why it failed: a well-documented trivial fact could out-score a plainly better one, because you were comparing things that aren't comparable.
  • New way: sort facts into four tiers first. The tier decides. The score only breaks ties inside a tier.

In plain terms: no amount of evidence lets a former employer beat something the person posted last week.

Why it never breaks when a key expires

  • Every outside service is optional. None of them is required.
  • No key, wrong key and out of credit all produce the same outcome: the run finishes on whatever's still working.
  • The only difference is the reason it shows you.
  • And nothing spends credit just to draw a status light. It only checks when a person clicks the button.

What money would buy

  • Verified email addresses. Right now it looks one up where it can and otherwise works it out from the company's naming pattern — and labels it as worked-out, everywhere it appears.
  • Job-posting and new-executive feeds. The two strongest buying signals there are, and the two that public search is worst at finding.
  • Writing straight into the CRM. Reps live in Outreach and HubSpot. A tool that needs copy-paste gets abandoned by week three.

Every decision above happened because something broke first. That's the honest version of an architecture diagram — not the plan that survived, but the shape left behind after it didn't.
